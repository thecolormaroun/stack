# Application examples

Use these illustrations to calibrate scope; apply the technique inventory selectively to other tasks.

## Depth with a fixed scope

Ask: “Explain churn from this quarterly summary. Keep it to one page.”

Choice: Depth, because a credible explanation requires checking the underlying evidence rather than polishing the summary. Expert addition: test the onboarding explanation against alternatives. Raw material: customer-level events, exposure and cancellation records. Methods: cohort retention and appropriately controlled comparisons. Outside check: reproducible results and a held-out period. Preserve the one-page deliverable; request unavailable raw data rather than pretending it exists.

Prompt: “Explain our churn in one page for the leadership team. Treat the quarterly summary as a starting hypothesis. If available and authorized, use customer-level event, exposure and cancellation records to define churn consistently, compare relevant cohorts, test the onboarding explanation against alternatives, and quantify uncertainty. Validate any predictive finding on a held-out period. Separate association from causation. If only the summary is available, identify what it supports and the smallest raw-data request needed to test the explanation. Deliver the strongest supported finding, its limits, and the next decision it informs.”

## Altitude and range without dropping a required artifact

Ask: “Create five slides to get a decision on next quarter's staffing.”

Choice: Altitude to center the staffing decision; range to combine workforce scenario analysis and decision-focused editing. Keep five slides. Do not assume permission to change staffing.

Prompt: “Create five slides that let leadership decide next quarter's staffing allocation. Use the supplied demand, capacity and cost information; flag missing inputs. Compare feasible options against our stated budget and priorities, show assumptions and trade-offs, and recommend a decision with conditions that would change it. Apply workforce scenario analysis to the options and editorial techniques to make the evidence and decision legible. End with the specific decision requested. Do not change staffing or contact anyone.”

## Speed with minimal expansion

Ask: “I have ten minutes. Turn these notes into a 150-word status update.”

Choice: Speed only; the output and time limit are clear. A deeper research project would get in the way.

Prompt: “Within ten minutes, turn only the supplied notes into a clear status update of no more than 150 words. Prioritize progress, blockers and next steps. Reuse the notes' facts, remove repetition, and flag rather than invent any missing material detail. Check names, dates and the word limit before returning one finished draft.”

## Creative work

Ask: “Improve my four-line poem without changing its voice.”

Choice: Depth, using close reading, sound, imagery and revision alternatives within the same four lines; avoid inventing a commercial goal. Where an objective correctness test does not exist, say so. Use read-aloud comparison and a relevant reader's feedback as limited external evidence, not proof of artistic correctness.

## All four, only with a fitting goal

Ask: “Improve this plan for a six-week onboarding experiment. We have a fixed budget and want evidence that activation improves.”

Possible choice: Altitude connects tasks to the activation decision; depth adds instrumentation, experimental design and held-out or prospective validation; range uses behavioral research and usability methods; speed reuses existing instrumentation and runs genuinely independent preparation in parallel. Preserve six weeks and the supplied budget. Propose changes for review; do not launch an experiment, collect personal data or enroll users merely because the prompt was rewritten.
